---
title: Six Thinking Hats
level: Advanced
tags: perspectives analysis
description: Look at a topic from six fixed modes of thinking in turn, each marked by a hat color.
---

The method comes from Edward de Bono (1985). Each hat stands for one mode of thinking that you adopt deliberately and on its own: white for facts, red for feelings, black for risks, yellow for benefits, green for new ideas, blue for managing the process. Instead of facts, doubts and hopes running together in one paragraph, each mode gets its own place.

## Example

```prompt
Topic: [Your topic, e.g. Our team of twelve is moving to a four-day week.]

Analyze the topic using Edward de Bono's six thinking hats, one after another. Write one paragraph per hat and stay within that hat's mode of thinking.

White hat: What facts and figures do we have, and which are missing?
Red hat: What feelings and gut reactions does the topic trigger, and in whom?
Black hat: What are the risks and weak points?
Yellow hat: What are the benefits and opportunities, and under what conditions do they materialize?
Green hat: What new ideas or alternatives are possible?

Finish with the blue hat: summarize what the five perspectives add up to, where they conflict, and what next step follows.
```

## When it helps

For decisions and discussions where one viewpoint crowds out the others, for example when a proposal is judged only on its risks or only with enthusiasm. The fixed sequence ensures that uncomfortable perspectives get a turn, and the closing summary turns them into a basis for a decision. The model plays every hat itself, so the red hat describes assumed reactions, not measured ones. To learn how your team actually feels, you have to ask them.
