The OODA loop comes from John Boyd, a fighter pilot and strategist in the US Air Force. It describes decision-making under time pressure as a loop: Observe, Orient, Decide, Act. After acting, the loop starts again with new observations. Boyd considered orientation the most important step, because that is where experience, assumptions and context determine what the observations mean.

## Example

```prompt
Situation: [e.g. A direct competitor cut its prices in half today.]
Context: [our product, our customers, our margins]

Take me through an OODA loop:
1. Observe: What do we know for certain, and what are we only assuming? Keep the two apart.
2. Orient: What does this mean for our customers, our brand and our numbers? Which of our assumptions could be wrong here?
3. Decide: Give three options with pros and cons, and recommend one.
4. Act: What is the first concrete step in the next 48 hours, and what observation will tell us afterwards whether it is working?
```

## When it helps

Fast-moving situations where you have to act quickly and things keep changing: responding to a competitor, operational disruptions, crisis communication. The question about the next observation closes the loop. For long-term strategy under stable conditions, more thorough methods fit better.
