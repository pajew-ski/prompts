title: Lateral Thinking
level: Advanced
tags: creativity ideas
description: The model looks for solutions that sidestep or reframe the problem instead of improving the obvious fix.
---

The term comes from Edward de Bono (1967). Vertical thinking develops an approach further by logic; lateral thinking questions the approach itself. Instead of improving the obvious solution, you ask whether the problem can be avoided, shifted or framed differently.

In the prompt you name the obvious solution and rule it out. Otherwise the model mostly offers variations of it. Assigning a different approach to each idea makes sure the suggestions really differ.

## Example

```prompt
Problem: [e.g. Our office does not have enough parking spaces for staff.]
Obvious solution: [e.g. build or rent more parking.] This solution and its variants are ruled out.

Find five solutions that sidestep or reframe the problem. Use a different approach for each:
1. Ask about the need behind the problem: who needs what, and why?
2. Reverse an assumption everyone makes without saying it.
3. Shift the problem in time or place.
4. Connect the problem with a random word: [e.g. library].
5. Consider who else could solve the problem for us.

Describe each solution in two sentences and name its biggest drawback.
```

## When it helps

When the obvious solutions are known and not good enough, in deadlocked discussions, and in the early ideas phase. Many suggestions will be unusable; that is part of the method. Evaluate them in a second step and keep working with the few that can be implemented. For problems with one clearly correct answer, such as arithmetic, it is the wrong tool.
